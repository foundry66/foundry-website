Appointing a Project Manager for Your Development: The Scope, the Questions, the Red Flags

What an owner's-side project manager actually does across a development, what to ask before you sign, and the warning signs worth walking away from.

27 October 2026 · 6 minute read

Type “project management company Dubai” into Google and the autocomplete finishes the sentence: services, consultancy, firms. People searching this are comparing who to appoint.

What most of the results will not tell them is what they are actually appointing, because the role is described by the firms selling it. This piece sets out what an owner's-side project manager does across a development, what to ask before you sign, and the red flags worth walking away from.

What the scope actually covers

An owner's project manager represents you across the whole arc of the project, and the early stages are where the money is really decided:

  1. Strategic definition and brief. What is this project, for whom, and what does success look like in numbers? The RIBA Plan of Work starts here, at Stage 0, before any designer is appointed. A weak brief is the most expensive mistake in development, because every later discipline prices off it.
  2. Feasibility and appraisal. Does the scheme stack up, and does the design serve the appraisal or the other way round? Your project manager should run the numbers before the pictures, and keep running them as the design evolves. We wrote about that order of work in Numbers Before Pictures.
  3. Assembling and managing the design team. Appointing the architect, engineers and specialists against the brief, coordinating their output, and keeping the design inside the budget it was appraised against.
  4. Procurement and contract. How the work is packaged and tendered, which contract forms are used, and where the risk sits. These choices lock in your exposure long before construction starts.
  5. Delivery oversight. Monitoring the contractor's progress, quality and claims against the contract, reporting to you in plain terms, and challenging early when something drifts.
  6. Handover and close-out. Defects, documentation, commissioning: the unglamorous end where value is protected or quietly leaks away.

The questions worth asking

Who will actually run my project day to day: the person in this room, or a junior team I have not met? Show me a live report you send clients: one page or forty? How do you get paid, and does anything you earn depend on the cost of my construction? What do you do in month three when the design is over budget: who tells me, and when?

A firm that answers these plainly is a firm that will report plainly.

The red flags

The fee is a percentage of construction cost with no cap, so every overrun pays the manager more. The “project manager” is really the contractor's man, and his first loyalty is to the build, not to you. The proposal leads with a portfolio of photographs and says nothing about briefs, budgets or appraisals; for high-end residential, cultural and resort work especially, the difference between a memorable building and an expensive lesson is set on paper before design starts. And the scope excludes the early stages, so the appointment begins after the decisions that mattered.

One real example, anonymised. On a complex resort project, the budget became available only at the end of concept design, after senior-level approval of a design that had not yet been priced. The project manager worked with the team through significant value engineering to bring the design within budget. The lesson: establish the budget early, and test the developing design against it before concept approval.

What good looks like

A good owner's project manager makes the numbers legible to you at every stage, tells you early when something is wrong, and makes fees and incentives clear from the outset.
