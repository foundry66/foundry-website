Why Master Plans Go Round in Circles

The feasibility and appraisal loop, and why the architect should get a firm brief before the pretty pictures are drawn.

30 September 2026 · 8 minute read

Many commercial developments in the United Arab Emirates start with the wrong thing: a beautiful master plan.

The numbers come later. Often they do not work. The design is sent back, redrawn and tested again. Time and fees are lost. Trust between developer and architect wears thin.

There is a better order of work.

  1. Test the idea with a light feasibility study.
  2. Test it again with a financial appraisal.
  3. Repeat the two, in a loop, until the scheme is viable.
  4. Only then give the architect a firm brief and let design lead.

This is not a new idea. It runs through the development literature, from Graaskamp's feasibility theory to the Royal Institute of British Architects Plan of Work. But it is often skipped in practice. This first essay explains why it matters and how to do it. Part 2 looks at phasing and at what the region's largest projects teach us.

How it is usually done, and the circle it creates

The common model in the UAE is simple. The developer appoints an architect to prepare a master plan. At the same time, it appoints an international property consultant to prepare a development appraisal.

The two pieces of work run side by side. They meet at the end.

Too often, the appraisal shows the plan does not stack up. Sales values are too low for the build cost. Too much land goes to roads and open space. The mix of uses does not match demand.

So the plan goes back to the architect. Areas are cut. Towers grow taller. The mix is changed. The appraisal is run again. Each loop can take weeks or months.

Everyone loses:

  • The developer pays for design that is thrown away and loses time in the market.
  • The architect is blamed for a scheme that was never given a sound brief.
  • The project ends up as a compromise, neither beautiful nor viable.

The root problem is not the architect or the consultant. It is the order of work. The appraisal is treated as a one-off check on a finished design, not as a tool that shapes the design.

What the literature says

The case for putting feasibility before design is well made. Five threads stand out.

1. Feasibility is about fit, not a single test

James Graaskamp, the founder of modern real estate feasibility teaching, saw a project as feasible only when it fits its context. His definition, as quoted by Miles, Eppli and Kummerow (1998), is:

"A desirable real estate development permits maximum satisfaction of the consumer with an affordable structure, while respecting environmental limits … and permitting both the public infrastructure providers and the private market developer to achieve cash solvency."

Note the words affordable structure. The building is one of the variables to be solved, not a fixed input handed to the valuer.

2. Development is staged, and the early stages repeat

The Urban Land Institute's textbook, Real Estate Development: Principles and Process (Miles, Berens, Eppli and Weiss), sets out an eight-stage model. The first three stages are inception of the idea, refinement of the idea, and the feasibility study. Formal commitment comes only after these. The authors stress that the idea is reshaped many times before any firm commitment is made.

3. The appraisal is very sensitive

Most development appraisals use the residual method: value on completion, minus all costs and profit, leaves the land value. The Royal Institution of Chartered Surveyors warns that "small variations in these inputs can cause relatively high variations in the residual output" (Royal Institution of Chartered Surveyors, 2019).

This matters. A master plan that looks almost right can still fail the appraisal by a wide margin. A single pass cannot find the viable answer. Only repeated testing can.

4. The brief comes before design

The Royal Institute of British Architects Plan of Work 2020 starts with Stage 0, Strategic Definition. Here the client builds a business case and tests options, including the option not to build at all. Stage 1, Preparation and Briefing, produces the Project Brief. The Plan states that feasibility studies at this stage are "not part of the design process" (Royal Institute of British Architects, 2020). Concept design starts only at Stage 2.

Barrett and Stanley's Better Construction Briefing (1999) makes a related point. Briefing is a process, built up over time with the client, not a single document.

5. Early decisions carry the most value

The architect Patrick MacLeamy showed this as two curves. The ability to influence cost is highest at the start and falls away. The cost of making a change starts low and rises steeply (UrbanNext).

Research by the Construction Industry Institute puts numbers on it. Effective front-end planning can improve capital cost by about 10 percent, shorten the programme by about 7 percent and reduce change orders by about 5 percent (Construction Industry Institute, 2006).

A regional reminder

The UAE has lived this lesson. After the 2008 financial crisis, Dubai's Real Estate Regulatory Agency reported that 217 real estate projects had been cancelled by May 2011, with 237 out of 450 reviewed projects on track (Emirates 24|7, June 2011). The causes differed from project to project. But the lesson is the same: the bigger the vision, the more carefully it must be tested before design commits to it.

A better way: the feasibility and appraisal loop

Feasibility and appraisal should not run side by side and meet once. They should talk to each other, again and again, until the scheme works.

The feasibility study asks what the site can hold. How much floor area? What mix of uses? What does the market want, and at what price? The design work here is deliberately simple: block models, plot ratios and yield tables. No renderings.

The financial appraisal asks whether that answer pays. It tests land value, build cost, sales or rental income, finance cost and profit. Crucially, it tests sensitivity. What if sales values drop 10 percent? What if the programme slips a year?

If the answer is no, the scheme is adjusted and tested again. The levers are many: the mix of uses, density, phasing, product type, specification, land price, or the deal structure itself.

Each loop is quick and cheap because no detailed design has been done. Several loops at this stage usually cost less than one redesign of a finished master plan.

If the answer is yes, the loop has produced something valuable: a firm, tested brief. Only now should the architect take the lead.

The sequence, stage by stage

The loop sits inside a clear sequence. Each stage ends with a decision gate. Nothing moves forward until the gate is passed.

Three points matter most.

  • The development manager leads the early stages. Someone must own the loop, bring in the cost consultant, valuer and planner, and hold the numbers together. This is the development manager's job, not the architect's.
  • The architect is involved early, but lightly. Simple massing and capacity studies need design skill. What they do not need is a full master plan or renderings.
  • The appraisal is checked again at concept stage. Good design will change things. Gate 4 confirms the scheme still works before large fees are spent on detailed design.

What this means for developers in the UAE

The fix is not complicated. It needs a change in the order of work, and someone to own it.

Appoint a development manager first

The development manager acts for the developer. They run the feasibility and appraisal loop, bring in the right specialists, and keep design and numbers in step. They write the brief and hold the project to it.

This role is common in the United Kingdom and Australia. It is still less common in the UAE, where the master planner and the property consultant are often appointed directly and separately.

Six practical steps

  1. Set the objectives before anything is drawn. Target return, budget ceiling, timescale and appetite for risk.
  2. Keep early design simple. Block models and yield tables, not renderings.
  3. Make the appraisal a working tool, not a one-off report. It should be updated with every option tested.
  4. Test the downside. Run the scheme at lower sales values, higher costs and a longer programme.
  5. Sign off a firm brief. Areas, mix, phasing, budget and quality level, agreed in writing.
  6. Then let the architect design. With a sound brief, the design team can put its full skill into making the project beautiful and successful.

Better for architects too

This approach is good for architects. A firm brief means less abortive work, fewer redesigns and a scheme that gets built. The architect's creativity goes into a project that can succeed, rather than into one that will be cut back.
