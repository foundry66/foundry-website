Phasing, and Why the Vision Must Follow the Economics

Seafront first or the plots at the back? How phasing changes feasibility, and what the region's giga-projects teach commercial developers.

30 September 2026 · 7 minute read

In Part 1 we argued that commercial developments should loop feasibility and appraisal until the scheme is viable, and only then brief the architect.

This second essay looks at two questions that follow. Which part of a master plan should be built and sold first? And what happens when the vision is allowed to lead the economics?

Phasing: sell the best first, or the hardest first?

Phasing is one of the strongest levers in the feasibility loop. The same master plan can make or lose money depending on the order in which it is built and sold.

The question every developer asks is simple. Do we launch with the seafront villas everyone wants? Or with the plots at the back that nobody wants?

The honest answer is: usually neither on its own.

The case for selling the best first

  • Sales are fast and prices are strong.
  • Early cash pays for roads, utilities and marine works.
  • Borrowing is lower and finance cost falls.
  • The brand launches with success.

The risk is giving away the project's future. The best land is sold before the place exists, at prices that do not yet reflect the community around it. The developer is left with the hardest plots at the end, when momentum may have faded. These unsold back plots become the "tail" that drags down the final return.

The case for selling the hardest first

  • The prime land is kept for later, when the place has matured and prices are higher.
  • The developer captures the value it creates.

The risk here is the opposite. Buyers do not want to buy into a building site with nothing around it. Sales are slow, borrowing is high and the launch can damage the brand. Some schemes never recover from a weak first phase.

The middle path: place first, prime last

Most successful master-planned communities follow a middle path.

Phase 1 creates the place. It delivers the entrance, the spine road and a visible amenity, such as a park, beach club, school or shops. It offers a spread of homes across price points to test real demand.

A small premium release sets the price. A limited number of prime units are sold early. This proves the top of the market and gives a benchmark for later phases, without giving away the whole frontage.

Later phases capture the uplift. Each phase is priced from what the last one achieved. The bulk of the prime frontage is held back until the place is established.

Weaker plots are lifted by what is next to them. Plots at the back sell better when they sit beside the school, the park or the shops.

What the theory says

Real options theory supports this. Sheridan Titman showed that vacant land is an option to build the best building later, and that more uncertainty makes waiting more valuable (American Economic Review, 1985). Each later phase is such an option. Geltner and de Neufville's Flexibility and Real Estate Valuation under Uncertainty (2018) shows how developers can plan and value this flexibility, including changing the mix of later phases as the market moves.

Why this matters in the UAE

  • Off-plan sales fund construction, under strict rules. Dubai's escrow law (Law No. 8 of 2007) ring-fences buyers' money for its own project (Hadef & Partners). In Abu Dhabi, withdrawals from a project escrow account have generally required at least 20 percent of construction to be complete. Rules updated in 2025 let qualifying developers draw earlier against a bank guarantee (A&O Shearman). Either way, each phase must be able to fund itself.
  • Waterfront infrastructure is costly and comes first. Breakwaters, beach works, canals and seawalls are often needed before any waterfront plot can be sold.
  • Markets move fast. Demand shifts with oil prices, visa and residency rules, and new supply nearby. Phasing lets the developer adjust the later product.

Test phasing inside the loop

Phasing cannot be judged on a single residual figure. The appraisal must be a phased cash flow. For each phasing option, it should show peak borrowing, time to break even, internal rate of return and the risk of an unsold tail.

StrategyEarly cashPeak borrowingValue capturedMain risk
Best firstHighLowLowUnsold tail, lost uplift
Hardest firstLowHighHigh, if it survivesWeak launch, slow sales
Place first, prime lastMediumMediumHighNeeds discipline and a strong first phase

The right answer depends on the site, the market and the developer's funding. That is exactly why it belongs in the feasibility loop, before the master plan is fixed.

Vision projects and commercial projects are different

Not every project should start with a spreadsheet.

A national museum, an opera house, a cultural centre or a landmark mosque is built for a bigger reason. Its purpose is identity, culture or national ambition. The client is usually the state. Success is not measured by a residual land value. Here it is right for design ambition to lead, and for the budget to follow the vision.

A commercial development is different. A residential community, a mixed-use district, a hotel or a retail scheme must pay back its investors and its lenders. It must sell or let in a real market. Beauty still matters, and good design adds value. But it has to work inside the economic limits of the project.

The mistake is to run a commercial project as if it were a vision project. Leading with renderings creates expectations the numbers cannot meet.

Vision projectCommercial project
Typical clientGovernment or a national bodyPrivate or listed developer
Measure of successIdentity, culture, public benefitReturn on investment, sales, rental income
What leadsDesign visionFeasibility and appraisal
Role of the budgetFollows the vision, within limitsSets the limits for the design
Right moment for full designEarlyAfter a viable, signed brief

Some projects sit in between, such as tourism destinations backed by government land or funding. For these, the developer should be clear about which parts are there for the vision and which must pay their way.

When the render sells the project

In the last decade, the wider region, and Saudi Arabia above all, went through a period led by the big idea. Striking computer-generated images launched projects on a scale the world had not seen. The images did their job. They won attention, investors and headlines.

Many of those projects are now being cut back, delayed or re-thought.

  • The Line at NEOM was presented as a 170 kilometre city for 9 million people. It has been reduced to a first phase of about 2.4 kilometres for around 300,000 residents. Reports cite engineering complexity, cost and delivery (AGBI, July 2026).
  • The Public Investment Fund has cut budgets across its portfolio companies since 2024, by between 20 and 60 percent, as oil prices fell. Saudi Arabia's investment minister said that "if they are less viable, the projects' scope may be reduced" (AGBI, February 2026).
  • The 2029 Asian Winter Games, planned for the Trojena mountain resort, were postponed with no new date (Dezeen, January 2026).

These are national vision projects, backed by one of the largest sovereign funds in the world. Even so, the economics caught up with the vision.

The lesson for commercial developers is stronger still. A private developer has no sovereign fund behind it. If the numbers do not work, there is no second chance.

The vision has to follow the economics, not the other way round.

A great image can sell a project. Only sound numbers can build it.
